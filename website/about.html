About

Who we are, what we built, and the methodological choices behind a digital humanities investigation of 683 City of Los Angeles civil service job bulletins.

Project overview

Who Gets In?

This project examines how the City of Los Angeles civil service system structures access to public employment. Using 683 official job bulletins published by the Los Angeles Personnel Department, we analyze how education requirements, work experience, salary ranges, and exam pathways shape who can enter and advance within city government. Our central question is whether a system designed around standardized merit actually produces equal access, or whether the structure of its requirements creates barriers that go largely unexamined.

As a digital humanities project, it sits at the intersection of computational text analysis and interpretive inquiry. The corpus gives us a large and uniform set of institutional documents. The humanistic lens asks what those documents mean, not only what they contain, and holds the analytical choices themselves accountable.

Technical description

Three levels, three sets of choices.

Our Digital Humanities project moves through three interconnected levels: sources, processing, and presentation. Each level involves deliberate choices that shape what questions can be asked, what patterns can be seen, and how meaning is communicated to an audience. Below, we describe each level and explain the reasoning behind our decisions.

01

Level 1

Sources

Our primary source is the City of Los Angeles Job Bulletins dataset, 683 plain text civil service job bulletins published by the Los Angeles Personnel Department. Each bulletin covers one job classification that contains its duties, minimum qualifications, salary range, and application process, spanning roles from administrative clerks and accountants to engineers, police officers, and zoo curators. We chose it because these are official government documents, primary sources that represent how the City formally defines and communicates employment opportunity, which makes them especially well suited for a project concerned with how access is structured through institutional language rather than simply described by it.

One limitation applies throughout. The bulletins describe official requirements and salary ranges, but they do not show who applied, who was hired, or how workers experienced the process. Our analysis is an analysis of how opportunity is defined, not of how it is actually lived. The full source description, scope, and critique are available on the Data page.

02

Level 2

Processing

We used Python with regex text extraction to transform 683 unstructured plain text files into a structured dataset of 21 defined fields, covering job title, salary range, minimum education requirement, required years of experience, and exam type. We then organized every job into one of five broad sectors: Public Safety, Administration, Technical/Engineering, Trades/Infrastructure, and Health/Social Services to enable meaningful comparison across job categories.

This sector classification was developed by our team for this project. It allows pattern analysis at a scale that would otherwise be impossible, but it necessarily simplifies the real differences between individual positions within each category. The complete cleaning and extraction pipeline is documented on the Data page.

03

Level 3

Presentation

We present our findings through a public website organized around five data visualizations, each paired with a research question and an extended interpretive discussion. Our website integrates narrative writing, data, and argument in a single publicly accessible space. It supports navigation that is not strictly sequential, but a structure that suits a project where each visualization contributes a distinct angle on a shared question rather than building a single linear case.

We paired each visualization with extended prose rather than letting the data stand alone, because the goal of this project is not only to display patterns but to make an argument about what those patterns mean for how public employment opportunity is structured.

Responsibilities

They were responsible for developing the research questions, writing the project narrative and visualization descriptions, and shaping the analytical argument across all sections of the site.
