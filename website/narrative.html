Chapter 03

Findings & Narrative

A project narrative followed by four visualizations: one argument about who the bulletins let in, told through salary, education, experience, and exam access.

Project narrative

A meritocracy on paper.

The City of Los Angeles civil service system is built around an important idea: public jobs should be available through a fair and organized process. The system uses standardized exams, listed qualifications, and public job bulletins to create a clear path for people who want to work in city government. These rules are designed to make hiring decisions based on skills and qualifications. However, having the same rules for everyone does not always mean that everyone experiences the system in the same way.

Our project studies how Los Angeles civil service jobs define qualifications and opportunities. We look at how education requirements, work experience, and promotion systems shape who can enter different types of jobs and how much these jobs pay. Education is often seen as an important measure of qualification, but our analysis suggests that higher education requirements do not always match with higher salaries across different job categories. Other factors, including job sectors and access to internal promotion opportunities, may also influence career paths.

To explore this question, we analyze about 683 official City of Los Angeles civil service job bulletins. We collect information about salary ranges, minimum education requirements, required years of experience, and exam types. Some exams are open-competitive, meaning that outside applicants can apply. Other exams are promotional, meaning that they are limited to current City employees. We also organize jobs into different areas, including public safety, administration, technical and engineering work, skilled trades, and health/social services.

This dataset gives us a way to study the rules and language used in real public job postings. Since these bulletins come directly from the City of Los Angeles, they show how jobs are officially described and how requirements are presented to applicants. However, this dataset also has limits. The bulletins show job requirements and salary information, but they do not show who applied, who was hired, or the personal experiences of workers. Because of this, our project focuses on how access is structured through job postings.

Access to public employment is an important topic because city jobs can provide stable income, benefits, and long-term career opportunities. For many communities in Los Angeles, government jobs have played an important role in creating economic stability. Looking at who qualifies for different positions can help us understand how these opportunities are organized and distributed.

Our project connects to previous research about hiring practices, job requirements, and inequality in employment. Many researchers have shown that job postings can influence who applies for certain positions. The way a job describes skills, experience, and qualifications can affect how people understand their own opportunities. Research on hiring language, recruitment, and workplace diversity also shows that employment systems can still create unequal results even when they use standardized processes.

Many existing studies focus on private companies, online hiring platforms, or individual applicants. Our project looks at a city government system, where official exams, job classifications, and promotion rules play a large role. These systems are often viewed as objective because they use written requirements and public processes. At the same time, the way these requirements are designed can affect who enters the system and who has opportunities to advance.

Through computational text analysis, we study patterns across Los Angeles civil service job bulletins. We ask how the system defines qualifications and rewards different types of work. We examine questions such as: How much does education affect salary? How does work experience relate to pay? How do internal promotion systems influence career opportunities?

Our findings suggest that salary and career opportunities are connected to multiple factors. Education and experience matter, but they do not explain every difference we see in the data. Job categories and access to promotion pathways also appear to play important roles. By studying these patterns, our project shows how a system based on merit can still contain hidden factors that shape who gets access to different opportunities.

Reading the chapter

The following sections connect our data visualizations with the main questions of this project. Together, these findings show how Los Angeles civil service opportunities are shaped by education, experience, job categories, and the rules that organize career movement within the system.

01

Average Annual Salary by City Job Classification Sector

Research question

How do average salaries differ across City of Los Angeles job sectors? What can these differences show about the relationship between job categories and economic opportunities within the civil service system?

Meaning in context

This visualization shows that salary levels vary across different types of City jobs. Public Safety and Technical/Engineering positions have some of the highest average salary midpoints in this dataset, while Administrative and Trades/Infrastructure positions appear lower. This pattern suggests that job sector may play an important role in shaping salary outcomes.

This finding connects to our larger question about whether qualifications alone explain differences within the civil service system. If education and experience were the only factors related to salary, we might expect jobs with similar requirements to have similar pay levels. However, the differences across sectors suggest that the type of work and the career structure surrounding each field may also influence compensation.

These salary differences may come from many factors, including required skills, certifications, training processes, labor agreements, job risks, and historical decisions about how different positions are organized. Because of this, salary should be understood as part of a larger employment system rather than only a reflection of individual qualifications.

This pattern matters because City jobs can provide important paths toward stable income and career growth. By examining which sectors have higher salary ranges, we can better understand how opportunities are distributed across different areas of public employment.

This visualization also has limitations. The chart compares salary by sector, but it does not include information about individual workers, applicants, or hiring outcomes. It cannot fully explain why these differences exist. The Health/Social Services category also contains only nine job bulletins, so we treat this result carefully and see it as a question for further research.

02

Average Annual Salary by Required Education Level

Research question

How do education requirements relate to average salaries in City of Los Angeles civil service jobs? Do positions requiring higher levels of formal education always have higher salary ranges, or do other factors also influence compensation?

Meaning in context

This visualization explores the relationship between education requirements and salary levels. The overall pattern shows that jobs requiring higher education often have higher average salaries, but the increase is not perfectly consistent across all categories.

If education level was the only factor connected to salary, we might expect the bars to increase evenly from no formal requirement to graduate degree. However, the data shows a more complex pattern. Jobs without a formal education requirement have a slightly higher average salary than jobs requiring a high school diploma or GED. Some of these positions may involve specialized skills, training programs, certifications, or work experience that are not measured through traditional education levels.

This finding connects to our larger question about how the civil service system defines qualifications. Education requirements can create important standards for certain positions, but they do not fully explain how salaries are organized across different jobs. Other factors, such as job sector, technical skills, licenses, and career pathways, may also shape compensation.

This pattern is important because education requirements influence who is eligible to apply for certain positions. When a degree requirement exists, it can affect access for applicants with different educational backgrounds. Understanding the relationship between education and salary helps us examine whether formal credentials consistently lead to greater economic opportunities within public employment.

At the same time, this visualization has limitations. The chart only compares stated education requirements and salary ranges from job bulletins. It does not include individual applicants, worker backgrounds, job performance, or final hiring outcomes. Because of this, the data helps us identify patterns in job requirements, but it cannot explain every reason behind salary differences.

03

Required Work Experience vs. Annual Salary Midpoint by Sector

Research question

How does required work experience relate to salary levels in City of Los Angeles civil service jobs? Does more required experience always connect with higher compensation, or do other factors also influence salary differences?

Meaning in context

This visualization explores whether years of required experience can explain salary differences across City jobs. The chart shows a general relationship between experience and salary, but the pattern is not completely consistent. Jobs with similar experience requirements can still have very different salary levels.

Some positions that require fewer years of experience have relatively high salary ranges, while some positions requiring more experience appear at lower salary levels. This suggests that experience requirements alone do not fully explain how compensation is organized across civil service jobs.

The differences between sectors provide another important layer of analysis. Public Safety and Technical/Engineering positions often appear in higher salary ranges, while many Administrative positions are concentrated at lower salary levels. This pattern suggests that job category, specialized skills, training requirements, and career structures may also contribute to salary differences.

This finding connects to our larger question about how the civil service system defines qualifications. Experience is an important part of many job requirements, but the data suggests that years of experience do not translate into the same salary outcomes across every field.

This visualization also has limitations. The chart only shows the minimum experience listed in job bulletins. It does not include information about individual workers, previous career paths, promotions, or hiring decisions. Because of this, the visualization helps identify relationships between experience and salary, but it cannot fully explain why those relationships exist.

04

Salary Midpoint Distribution: Promotional vs. Open-Competitive Exams by Sector

Research question

How do salary distributions differ between promotional and open-competitive civil service positions? What can these differences show about the role of internal career pathways in the City of Los Angeles employment system?

Meaning in context

This visualization examines how different exam pathways relate to salary opportunities within the civil service system. While the system uses standardized exams and public requirements, different types of exams may create different career pathways. Across several job sectors, promotional positions tend to have higher salary midpoints than open-competitive positions.

Across several job sectors, promotional positions tend to have higher salary midpoints than open-competitive positions. This pattern suggests that internal career movement may be connected to access to higher-paying roles within the City system.

This finding helps us understand that career opportunities are shaped by more than initial qualifications. After entering the civil service system, employees may gain access to additional opportunities through internal promotions, accumulated experience, and career advancement structures.

When compared with the education and experience visualizations, this chart adds another layer to our research question. Salary differences are related to multiple factors, including education, experience, job sector, and access to different exam pathways. These factors work together to shape how people move through public employment systems.

However, this visualization also has limitations. The data shows salary differences between exam types, but it does not show individual career histories or explain why specific employees move into promotional roles. It also does not include applicant backgrounds or hiring results. Because of this, we interpret promotional access as one important factor connected to salary patterns rather than the only explanation.

Conclusion

What the bulletins say about access.

Key takeaways

  • →Job sector appears to be an important factor related to salary differences across City of Los Angeles civil service positions. Education level alone does not explain all salary patterns in the dataset.
  • →Higher education requirements are generally connected with higher salaries, but the relationship is not perfectly linear across all education levels. Formal credentials influence access to certain positions, but they do not always predict compensation by themselves.
  • →Required work experience relates to salary in different ways across job sectors. The same number of required years does not always lead to the same salary outcomes.
  • →Promotional exam pathways tend to have higher salary distributions than open-competitive pathways, especially in sectors such as Public Safety and Technical/Engineering.
  • →Together, these patterns suggest that civil service opportunities are shaped by multiple factors, including education, experience, job categories, and internal career pathways.

Limitations

  • — Job bulletins describe official requirements and salary ranges, but they do not show final hiring decisions or employee experiences.
  • — The dataset does not include individual applicant information, exam scores, demographic data, or hiring records.
  • — Snapshot in time; salaries are not CPI-adjusted across publication dates.
  • — Five-sector classification is a researcher choice and condenses real heterogeneity.
  • — Health / Social Services subsample (n = 9) is too thin for confident claims.
